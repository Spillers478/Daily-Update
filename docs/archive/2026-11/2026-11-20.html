Soaking Storms Hit Florida’s Space Coast, Prompting Human and Horse Rescues
The Manila Times ·

COCOA, Florida — Storms that brought record rains to South Florida this week shifted north to the Space Coast on Thursday, sending rivers over their banks and prompting water rescues of both humans and horses alike. In California, rain continued to pound the coast, filling streets with water, submerging cars and forcing hundreds of flight cancellations. This is supposed to be Florida’s dry season that runs from October to May. So far, it has been quite the opposite. “Never seen such a thing like this in Florida,” said Jazmin Foster, who has lived in her Melbourne home for eight years. The storms have already dumped nearly 17 inches of rain over 48 hours in the Fort Lauderdale area, according to preliminary totals from the National Weather Service. Places like Fort Lauderdale, Melbourne and West Palm Beach have already seen their wettest November on record, the National Weather Service said. This week’s storms have been fueled by persistent high pressure over Virginia and a low-pressure system and an associated stationary front in the Gulf, which together have pushed a steady onshore flow across the Florida Peninsula, the weather service said. Some South Florida school districts were closed for a second day Thursday. The heavy rains and flash flooding were expected to persist through Friday, possibly moving north toward coastal portions of the Carolinas and the mid-Atlantic into the weekend, the weather service said. Dozens of horses and other animals caught in floodwaters at equestrian center On Thursday, the storms hit Brevard County directly. People with horse trailers braved knee-deep water to rescue dozens of horses and other animals at the Blue Lake Equestrian Center, an animal hospice and boarding facility in Cocoa. The animals were taken to a dry pasture elsewhere. First responders conducted nearly 100 high-water rescues across the county, and those numbers continued to climb, Sheriff Wayne Ivey said. The city of Palm Bay conducted mandatory evacuations for some residents of a mobile home community. Shelters were opened for residents forced from their homes. Water inundated Foster's home Thursday, two weeks after she endured a previous round of flooding. She said an insurance claim filed for the earlier flood was denied. “Now this time we have standing water in our yard, but not as much, but our road was flooded and sewers overflowing and more water coming in my two kids’ bedrooms again,” Foster told The Associated Press via social media. “Where is FEMA? Multiple people here in Brevard need help! I know other people are worse than we are!” Nearly 200 Florida National Guard personnel with 50 high-water vehicles have been deployed to help, the Division of Emergency Management said. Other responding agencies included about 235 officers with the Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission. The St. Johns River near Cocoa rose above a record flood level set in 1953 and was forecast to stay at least 2 feet above flood stage through early next week, according to the weather service. Cyndy Paulin saw a fish swimming on her flooded street in Cocoa on Thursday, and her adventurous 5-year-old granddaughter played in the water. She brought a small body board and got on it, but it immediately sank to the ground, and she got soaked. “She had a blast,” Paulin said. El Niño isn’t going away anytime soon The storms are an offshoot of El Niño, the natural warming of the Pacific near the equator that warps weather patterns worldwide. Forecasters said El Niño conditions are expected to persist through the spring, meaning the soggy extreme weather in Florida and California will likely continue through the winter. “That’s what the forecast models are saying,” said Yale Climate Connections meteorologist Jeff Masters. “Winter hasn’t even come yet — we’re still in November. And things are likely to get even more active. And with record-warm ocean temperatures both off the coast of California and off the coast of Florida, we’re going to be seeing some very heavy downpours.” In July, United Nations officials warned that El Niño would add fuel to the world’s accelerating climate crisis, powered by the burning of coal, oil and natural gas. Longtime Pompano Beach resident Ken Evans said he has seen heavier rain and flooding worsen, especially over the past five years. On Tuesday night, he was caught in his SUV in a heavy downpour with hail on Interstate 95 north of Miami, plunging many roads under water. “This is absolutely a whole new cycle that’s going on, and a cycle that we should be very concerned about,” Evans said. California endures more flooding Some areas of Southern California saw 1 to 2 inches of rainfall within an hour Thursday, the weather service said. A flash-flood warning was issued for southwestern Los Angeles County, including Malibu, Pacific Palisades and the Hollywood Hills. More than 200 flights to and from Los Angeles International Airport were delayed Thursday, about 12% of the total air traffic, but there were only a handful of cancellations, according to Flightradar24. Evacuation warnings for the Sunset, Hearst and Palisades burn scar areas in Los Angeles County remained in effect until Friday morning, because of heavy rains and possible debris flows. High surf, dangerous waves, rip currents, beach erosion and coastal flooding were forecast along the southern and central California coast. Some piers could also sustain damage. South of the Monterey Peninsula, heavy rains spurred rock and mud slides in Big Sur, forcing a more than 40-mile (64-kilometer) closure of Highway 1 along the Pacific coast. There were no immediate reports of injuries. Watching the skies — and surf — for Thanksgiving week A nontropical low-pressure system expected to form Friday could become a subtropical or tropical storm along the southeastern U.S. coast, the National Hurricane Center in Miami said. The southeast and mid-Atlantic coasts should expect strong winds, dangerous surf and coastal flooding into early next week. In Southern California, the weather service said extremely high tides of more than 8 feet (2.4 meters) were expected to continue through next week.

World: Children affected by armed conflict, 1990–2025
Reliefweb reassigned from Military Conflict·

Please refer to the attached file. More children than ever live in conflict zones Posted Thursday, 19 Nov 2026 by Gudrun Østby & Siri Aas Rustad More children now live near armed conflict than the combined populations of the United States, Canada and Mexico. Armed violence escalated sharply across the globe in 2025. Data from the Uppsala Conflict Data Program (UCDP) shows that conflict-related fatalities rose to 244,600, up from approximately 187,000 in 2024. This made 2025 one of the deadliest years since the end of the Cold War. Much of the increase was driven by a dramatic rise in violence targeting civilians, particularly in Sudan. While armed conflict affects entire populations, children are among those who suffer the most severe and lasting consequences. Children face grave violations, including killing and maiming, recruitment by armed groups, sexual violence, abduction, attacks on schools and hospitals, and denial of humanitarian access. But the consequences of war extend far beyond direct violence. Children living in conflict-affected areas are more likely to drop out of school, lack access to clean water, and face heightened risks of death from illness, malnutrition, or lack of vaccines and medical care. Today, Save the Children launches its new report, Stop the War on Children 2026. The report is based on PRIO’s tenth annual mapping of children in armed conflict covering the period 1990–2025. Our latest estimates show that 589 million children, more than one in five of the world's children, were living in conflict zones in 2025. This represents a 7% increase from 2024, when an estimated 550 million children were affected. It marks a new all-time high both in absolute numbers and as a share of the global child population. While the figures have fluctuated from year to year, the long-term trend is clear. The proportion of children living in conflict zones has more than doubled since the mid-1990s, rising from around 10% to 23.4% in 2025. How we estimate the number of conflict-affected children Most conflicts occur within limited, localized areas of countries. To estimate the number of children living in conflict zones, it is essential to isolate these areas. The UCDP Georeferenced Events Dataset (UCDP GED) provides precise data on the location of each separate conflict event or incidence of organized violence, including geographical coordinates. Using this data, we map circles with a 50 km radius around each conflict event and estimate the number of children residing within these areas based on population data from CIESIN and the United Nations. We define children affected by conflict as those living within 50 km of one or more conflict events. This 50 km radius is considered a reasonable distance within which conflict can significantly disrupt children's daily lives. Key findings from the latest update An estimated 1.5 billion children lived in a conflict-affected country in 2025. Of these, 589 million, more than one in five children worldwide, lived in a conflict zone. 355 million children lived in high-intensity conflict zones (with 1,000 or more battle-related deaths) in 2025. This represents an increase of more than 11% from 319 million in 2024. Africa had the largest number of children living in conflict zones, with 260 million. The Middle East had the highest share relative to its child population, at 40.2%. What can be done to protect children in conflict zones? Now, more than ever, the need to protect millions of children in conflict zones is urgent. While the devastating impacts of war in Ukraine and the Middle East continue to demand sustained international attention, the growing concentration of conflict-affected children in Africa underscores the need for greater focus on the continent. Africa is now home to 44% of the world's conflict-affected children, and humanitarian needs continue to rise rapidly. Addressing the growing impact of armed conflict on children worldwide will require sustained commitment from governments, donors, humanitarian organizations, and local communities. Priorities include: Invest in research and data collection to better understand how armed conflict affects children. Ensure adequately resourced and high-quality peacekeeping operations in conflict-affected areas. Partner with local peacebuilders and place children's needs at the centre of peace processes from the outset. Establish and protect safe spaces where children can learn, play, and access essential services. Strengthen accountability mechanisms, including credible sanctions against armed groups responsible for grave violations against children. Increase support to conflict-affected countries to rebuild essential infrastructure, particularly education and health systems, at a time when aid budgets are under growing pressure. For more information, read Save the Children’s full 2026 report, Stop the War on Children 2026. The exercise will focus on coordinated operations in jungle and semi-urban terrain, incorporating joint planning, tactical coordination and the employment of emerging technologies to enhance interoperability and operational effectiveness. The exercise will be conducted in progressive phases, beginning with cross-training and familiarisation with each other's operational procedures, followed by joint tactical drills. Training activities will include establishment of a Joint Command Post and Surveillance Centre, counter-terrorism operations, raids, cordon-and-search operations and combat engineering drills. Special emphasis will be placed on the integrated employment of Unmanned Aerial Systems (UAS) for reconnaissance and surveillance, along with Counter-Unmanned Aerial System (C-UAS) capabilities to detect and neutralise hostile drones. The exercise will culminate in a joint validation exercise involving a simulated Counter-Terrorism scenario, incorporating coordinated ground operations, integrated surveillance and employment of unmanned systems. Exercise HARIMAU SHAKTI will provide an opportunity for both Armies to exchange best practices in tactics, techniques and procedures, while enhancing interoperability and mutual understanding. How stories are ranked and compared

Articles about the same event are grouped into one story by text similarity, across topics. Each story is scored 0–100 from five parts: independent-source corroboration (30%), source quality (20%), recency (15%), escalation and casualty language (25%), and the topic’s severity (10%). Syndicated copies and republisher sites count as one source, not many. Only stories whose best source is an established outlet or better can appear in Top developments. Stories are never hidden for their source — they are ranked lower.

Source categories are functional, not political:

  • wire primary · weight 1
  • established · weight 0.7
  • press release · weight 0.4
  • state affiliated · weight 0.3
  • advocacy opinion · weight 0.3
  • aggregator · weight 0.15
  • unclassified · weight 0.4

This is an automated heuristic. Grouping is approximate (occasionally two events are merged or one is split), scores measure how much and how credibly something is being reported, not how true or important it ultimately is, and trend arrows reflect news coverage volume, not events on the ground.
