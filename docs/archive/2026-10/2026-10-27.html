Excellency Mr. George Gerapetritis, President of the Security Council, Excellencies, Distinguished colleagues, For a child caught in armed conflict, the test of three decades of international commitments, including this Council’s resolutions, is whether these commitments change what happens next: whether monitors can reach children, whether they are recruited or released, whether they can return to school, and whether anyone is held to account when they are harmed. These are the tests we should put before ourselves today. Graça Machel’s 1996 report placed the experiences and rights of children at the centre of the international response to armed conflict. Successive Special Representatives have carried that work forward, bringing verified evidence before the Security Council, engaging parties, and pressing for changes in their conduct. The Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), its Optional Protocol on the Involvement of Children in Armed Conflict (OPAC), and international humanitarian law (IHL) instruments remain indispensable in guiding our collective efforts to prevent grave violations against children, ensure accountability, and uphold the rights and dignity of every child caught in situations of armed conflict. Over the past three decades, this combination of legal frameworks, sustained advocacy and engagement with parties to conflict has produced concrete results. More than 40 action plans have been signed to date with parties to conflict, contributing to the release of over 220,000 children from armed forces and groups and their transfer to civilian child-protection actors. More than 220,000 children. That figure alone speaks to the impact of this mandate, But its contribution extends beyond release. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, implementation of the action plan helped put measures in place to end and prevent the recruitment and use of children by the national armed forces. In Nigeria, more than 2,000 children formerly associated with the Civilian Joint Task Force were formally separated and received reintegration support. In Colombia, a national programme has supported thousands of children leaving armed groups to rebuild their lives in civilian communities. In some cases, verified implementation has also enabled parties to be delisted from the annexes of the Secretary General’s annual report. Again here, these are not just words. They represent commitments made with the UN and implemented. They represent children given a second chance in life, families reunited, and communities beginning to heal. Ultimately, they are the building blocks of lasting and sustainable peace. These are no small achievements in a climate where even principled engagement can be contested. Each action plan requires continued engagement, careful verification and practical follow-through. They show that persistent diplomacy, grounded in credible evidence, can change parties’ conduct and children’s lives. The impact of the mandate extends beyond action plans. The Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism (MRM) provides a credible, United Nations-verified picture of grave violations and helps identify patterns and emerging risks. Its findings have supported accountability efforts and informed consideration of targeted measures, including sanctions. Through advocacy and technical engagement with national authorities, the Office has also supported legal and policy changes, including measures to criminalise recruitment and use, protect schools from attack and ensure children born in captivity can be registered. National legislation and policy also show how these commitments can be put into practice. How stories are ranked and compared

Articles about the same event are grouped into one story by text similarity, across topics. Each story is scored 0–100 from five parts: independent-source corroboration (30%), source quality (20%), recency (15%), escalation and casualty language (25%), and the topic’s severity (10%). Syndicated copies and republisher sites count as one source, not many. Only stories whose best source is an established outlet or better can appear in Top developments. Stories are never hidden for their source — they are ranked lower.

Source categories are functional, not political:

  • wire primary · weight 1
  • established · weight 0.7
  • press release · weight 0.4
  • state affiliated · weight 0.3
  • advocacy opinion · weight 0.3
  • aggregator · weight 0.15
  • unclassified · weight 0.4

This is an automated heuristic. Grouping is approximate (occasionally two events are merged or one is split), scores measure how much and how credibly something is being reported, not how true or important it ultimately is, and trend arrows reflect news coverage volume, not events on the ground.
