World: Statement by Vanessa Frazier, Special Representative for Children and Armed Conflict to the UN Security Council (26 October 2026)
Reliefweb reassigned from Military Conflict·

Excellency Mr. George Gerapetritis, President of the Security Council, Excellencies, Distinguished colleagues, For a child caught in armed conflict, the test of three decades of international commitments, including this Council’s resolutions, is whether these commitments change what happens next: whether monitors can reach children, whether they are recruited or released, whether they can return to school, and whether anyone is held to account when they are harmed. These are the tests we should put before ourselves today. Graça Machel’s 1996 report placed the experiences and rights of children at the centre of the international response to armed conflict. Successive Special Representatives have carried that work forward, bringing verified evidence before the Security Council, engaging parties, and pressing for changes in their conduct. The Convention on the Rights of the Child (CRC), its Optional Protocol on the Involvement of Children in Armed Conflict (OPAC), and international humanitarian law (IHL) instruments remain indispensable in guiding our collective efforts to prevent grave violations against children, ensure accountability, and uphold the rights and dignity of every child caught in situations of armed conflict. Over the past three decades, this combination of legal frameworks, sustained advocacy and engagement with parties to conflict has produced concrete results. More than 40 action plans have been signed to date with parties to conflict, contributing to the release of over 220,000 children from armed forces and groups and their transfer to civilian child-protection actors. More than 220,000 children. That figure alone speaks to the impact of this mandate, But its contribution extends beyond release. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, implementation of the action plan helped put measures in place to end and prevent the recruitment and use of children by the national armed forces. In Nigeria, more than 2,000 children formerly associated with the Civilian Joint Task Force were formally separated and received reintegration support. In Colombia, a national programme has supported thousands of children leaving armed groups to rebuild their lives in civilian communities. In some cases, verified implementation has also enabled parties to be delisted from the annexes of the Secretary General’s annual report. Again here, these are not just words. They represent commitments made with the UN and implemented. They represent children given a second chance in life, families reunited, and communities beginning to heal. Ultimately, they are the building blocks of lasting and sustainable peace. These are no small achievements in a climate where even principled engagement can be contested. Each action plan requires continued engagement, careful verification and practical follow-through. They show that persistent diplomacy, grounded in credible evidence, can change parties’ conduct and children’s lives. The impact of the mandate extends beyond action plans. The Monitoring and Reporting Mechanism (MRM) provides a credible, United Nations-verified picture of grave violations and helps identify patterns and emerging risks. Its findings have supported accountability efforts and informed consideration of targeted measures, including sanctions. Through advocacy and technical engagement with national authorities, the Office has also supported legal and policy changes, including measures to criminalise recruitment and use, protect schools from attack and ensure children born in captivity can be registered. National legislation and policy also show how these commitments can be put into practice. For instance, in the Philippines, the Department of Education has adopted a national policy framework on learners and schools as zones of peace. The Office contributes CAAC analysis to national review processes, including the Universal Periodic Review and Voluntary National Reviews. Furthermore, over the years, more than ten country situations have been removed from the Secretary-General’s reporting following an assessment that conditions changed and child-protection concerns were addressed. Continued monitoring and follow-through remain essential to sustaining these gains. President, The scale of current violations remains stark. In 2025, the UN verified 38,558 grave violations affecting 24,174 children. This is the highest number of children affected since this mandate was established. For the first time in the mandate’s history, government forces were responsible for the majority of verified grave violations. This is deeply troubling. I condemn all grave violations against children, in every situation and every conflict, irrespective of who commits them. International humanitarian law and international human rights law bind all parties to an armed conflict. No party is exempt from its duty to protect children or from scrutiny when violations are committed. Over the past three decades, the Security Council has adopted 15 resolutions on children and armed conflict, establishing a strong framework that responds to both existing and emerging challenges. Resolution 2832, adopted unanimously just last week, adds to this body of work. I congratulate Greece on its leadership and thank Council members for their support. Member States have reinforced this robust framework through additional commitments such as the Paris and Vancouver Principles and the Safe Schools and EWIPA declarations. The Office has also developed child-protection guidance for European Union and NATO operations, works with regional organisations to strengthen protection efforts, and trains government officials, human rights practitioners, civil society, military personnel and mediators. The Office has also contributed child-protection expertise to international legal and policy processes, including the ongoing work on a Convention on Crimes Against Humanity. The Groups of Friends on children and armed conflict also help maintain political attention and support implementation. Through my Office’s “Prove It Matters” campaign, Member States can reaffirm their commitment to child protection and the Convention on the Rights of the Child. I encourage those who have not yet done so to join the campaign and translate their support into action for children affected by armed conflict. Since taking up this post, I have pursued a field-focused approach grounded in dialogue, diplomacy and action. I have so far undertaken missions to various situations and countries, including the Syrian Arab Republic, Lebanon, Haiti, Ukraine, and the Russian Federation. Missions and engagement with various Member States have been directed towards securing access, commitments and practical protection for children. I intend to continue these efforts in the period ahead. My Office has also issued supplementary analysis of the drivers and trends behind grave violations to support earlier and more effective action. Regional cooperation is equally important. I value my close work with the African Union Special Envoy for Children Affected by Armed Conflict, who is also briefing the Council today. In Cameroon, my Office’s efforts at a regional conference led to the technical endorsement of the Yaoundé Strategy - the first regional strategy for preventing violations against children in armed conflict in Central Africa. We intend to build on this progress and encourage similar efforts elsewhere. Field engagement brings us closer to children’s realities. Dialogue and diplomacy can open doors. Our responsibility is to use those openings to secure change. Engagement must never be a substitute for action. As we mark this anniversary, I urge the Council and Member States to focus on four priorities. First, ensure that verified information leads to action. Parties to conflict must allow monitors to work safely and without obstruction. When violations are documented, Member States should press for specific steps to end them, protect children and hold perpetrators accountable. The UN’s effort to monitor and respond to grave violations gives these children something priceless: visibility, protection, and the promise that the world has not forgotten them. Second, translate existing commitments into practice. Parties to conflict must protect schools from attack and refrain from using them for military purposes. States that have endorsed the Safe Schools Declaration and the Political Declaration on the Use of Explosive Weapons in Populated Areas (EWIPA) should implement their commitments through national policies, military guidance and operational procedures that reduce harm to children. They should also strengthen efforts to prevent recruitment and support children’s release and reintegration. Third, preserve Council unity and make full use of the Council’s toolkit. This framework has an impact only when Council members apply it consistently across situations and follow through on their decisions. I urge members to keep the CAAC Working Group’s work moving, adopt timely and practical conclusions, and maintain sustained engagement with parties. The Council’s voice carries weight when it speaks with unity, and acts on its decisions. Fourth, sustain prevention, protection and reintegration. Prevention depends on early action, capable child protection services and reliable support for monitoring and reintegration. Member States should sustain that work at local, national and regional levels. Children should also be heard safely and meaningfully in decisions about their protection and future. I also acknowledge UNICEF, UN peace operations and other UN entities contributing in the implementation of the CAAC mandate, Watchlist on Children and Armed Conflict, and the civil society organisations, national partners and child protection colleagues who work alongside children and communities, often in difficult and dangerous conditions. Their knowledge, access and persistence are essential to monitoring violations, preventing harm and helping children recover. They must be able to carry out this work safely and with the support it requires. Shockingly, 136 UN personnel, civilian and uniformed, and 350 humanitarian workers died in 2025. This is unacceptable. Thirty years of work have produced practical tools and measurable gains. Yet the continued scale of violations shows that the Council, Member States and parties to conflict must do more to protect children. These four priorities require more than consensus in this chamber - they require sustained political commitment. Protecting children is a global responsibility. Let us work together in unison for a world where justice, safety and dignity for children are not privileges but guarantees. Political commitment must also be matched by resources. I thank Member States and partners whose voluntary contributions support this mandate, and call for predictable, flexible, multi-year funding to sustain monitoring, prevention and reintegration. I remain fully committed, with energy and resolve, to working with all of you - Member States, parties to conflict, regional organisations, civil society and children themselves - to deliver on the mandate entrusted to me. I close with the words of a child who participated in the Prove It Matters campaign who told us: "War took both of my parents from me and left my childhood incomplete. No child should ever experience the pain I have lived through. Let us build our future not on loss, but on peace and compassion." Every day, I think of the children who wake up to conflict instead of classrooms, to fear instead of futures. This must change. Collective action can change this. Let us prove, through our actions, that children’s safety, dignity and future truly matter. Thirty years from now, children must be safer because we acted today. I am ready to do my part. I ask this Council to do its part too – use every tool at its disposal to protect children. I thank you.

How stories are ranked and compared

Articles about the same event are grouped into one story by text similarity, across topics. Each story is scored 0–100 from five parts: independent-source corroboration (30%), source quality (20%), recency (15%), escalation and casualty language (25%), and the topic’s severity (10%). Syndicated copies and republisher sites count as one source, not many. Only stories whose best source is an established outlet or better can appear in Top developments. Stories are never hidden for their source — they are ranked lower.

Source categories are functional, not political:

  • wire primary · weight 1
  • established · weight 0.7
  • press release · weight 0.4
  • state affiliated · weight 0.3
  • advocacy opinion · weight 0.3
  • aggregator · weight 0.15
  • unclassified · weight 0.4

This is an automated heuristic. Grouping is approximate (occasionally two events are merged or one is split), scores measure how much and how credibly something is being reported, not how true or important it ultimately is, and trend arrows reflect news coverage volume, not events on the ground.
