Warnings that worked The report, Global Status of Multi-Hazard Early Warning Systems 2026, finds that countries with substantial to comprehensive systems record disaster-related mortality 6.1 times lower than those with limited to moderate ones, and 3.2 times fewer people affected. It also shows what that means on the ground. When a magnitude 8.8 earthquake struck off Russia’s Kamchatka Peninsula in July 2025, the Pacific Tsunami Warning Center issued its first threat messages within 10 minutes. Japan evacuated approximately 1.9 million people, while in Ecuador’s Galápagos islands, more than 2,300 people were moved out of high-risk areas hours before waves of about 1.3 metres reached the shore. In Madagascar, an early action plan developed by the Malagasy Red Cross was partially activated before Tropical Cyclone Gezani made landfall in February, with some 600 door-to-door visits reaching 80,000 to 100,000 people with warnings and evacuation support. Since 2022, some $2 billion has been mobilised and around 160 countries are receiving support, he noted. But finance must cover the entire warning chain, he said; “from weather forecasting to communication tools to volunteers knocking on doors”. Warnings are also increasingly triggering action before disaster strikes: in 2025, 146 anticipatory action activations in 54 countries reached 9.6 million people, the report found. “Countries have made significant progress in strengthening their early warning systems, but too many people remain unprotected,” said Kamal Kishore, head of UNDRR. “The climate crisis is accelerating. Our protection of people must accelerate with it,” Mr. Guterres said. “Together, let us make sure that every warning arrives in time.”

NGAL’s capability attributes are expected to be sorted out during the AOA process, leaders said during the Airlift/Tanker Association’s annual conference last week, noting all options are on the table. “We're not just talking about platforms here, right? Strategic airlift is not just a flight; it's a cycle,” Col. Derrick McClain, AMC deputy director of strategy, plans, requirements and programs, said during a panel discussion here. “You’ve got to take off after you unload the cargo. You've got to fly to your destination, land, offload, onload -- potentially under threat,” he said. “And if the flight time is less than the ground time, that's a missed opportunity.” The upcoming NGAL AOA will assess constraints to onload and offload times, according to McClain, and consider a range of solutions from a new design to the modification of an existing platform. The Air Force is weighing reopening Boeing’s C-17 production line, which has been closed since 2015, as part of its analysis. “I think [NGAL] needs to be something that is as dependable or more so than the C-17, and that's a tall order, right? But it may not be as easy a solution as it sounds: restarting C-17 production could prove to be too expensive a feat to get any support, a congressional aide told Inside Defense on the sidelines of the conference on the condition of anonymity. There are also concerns with the C-17 engine. One airman during the panel asked McClain how viable restarting the line could be since the program has an extremely limited number of spares. The airman described instances in which maintainers had to cannibalize engine components to complete repairs. “As we go into the analysis of alternatives for NGAL, we're absolutely going to be looking at what kind of engines are required to set the enterprise,” McClain said during a question-and-answer session. “In that space of the mid-class thrust engine, there are less options than small and large class, and so we're talking about that.” Other possible NGAL ideas include a blended-wing body style airlifter, Tulley said, or an aircraft which requires only a single pilot and uses some artificial intelligence to assist with some basic tasks in the cockpit. “There are a number of characteristics and attributes that we want in that next-gen airlifter. How stories are ranked and compared

Articles about the same event are grouped into one story by text similarity, across topics. Each story is scored 0–100 from five parts: independent-source corroboration (30%), source quality (20%), recency (15%), escalation and casualty language (25%), and the topic’s severity (10%). Syndicated copies and republisher sites count as one source, not many. Only stories whose best source is an established outlet or better can appear in Top developments.   • established · weight 0.7
  • press release · weight 0.4
  • state affiliated · weight 0.3
  • advocacy opinion · weight 0.3
  • aggregator · weight 0.15
  • unclassified · weight 0.4
